## quality
The training file has 50,306 tweets with no missing ID, label or text, and the three expected labels. Repetitions are more frequent than they look: 686 rows are exact duplicates, 772 IDs and 777 texts appear more than once, and **84 IDs / 87 texts carry contradictory labels** (the same tweet annotated neutral and negative, for instance). These conflicts are annotation noise: no model can predict both labels. The descriptive statistics keep every row, but the models are trained on `deduplicate(train)`, which removes the conflicting tweets, keeps one copy of the others (so that a tweet cannot be both in the training and validation folds) and drops the one-character tweet "0". About 37 % of the raw texts were changed by the encoding repair, which is why the first version of this notebook showed `u002c` and `u2019` among the most frequent words and tweets up to 200 characters long (the repaired maximum is 141, consistent with the 140-character limit of the period).

## classes
The training set is imbalanced: neutral 44.9 %, positive 39.6 %, negative only 15.6 %. **The test set has a different balance**: negative tweets are twice as frequent (32.3 %) and positive ones half as frequent (19.3 %). The tweet IDs (Twitter "snowflake" IDs encode the posting time) show that the test tweets were all posted in November-December 2016 (topics such as #BlackLivesMatter or the aftermath of the US election), while the training tweets range from 2011 to December 2015. Two consequences for Task 2: accuracy is a poor metric (a model that rarely predicts "negative" would lose many test points while looking fine on the training distribution), so we report macro-F1 and macro-recall; and class weights matter, because a model fitted to the training priors would under-predict the negative class on the test set.

## lengths
Negative tweets are slightly longer (median 127 characters, 21 words) than neutral (125, 20) and positive ones (121, 20). The Kruskal-Wallis test is significant (p < 1e-38) only because the sample is large: the effect size ε² is below 0.006, i.e. the class explains less than 1 % of the variation in length. Length alone cannot separate the classes.

## words
Many frequent words are shared by the three classes and reflect how the data was collected rather than sentiment: *tomorrow*, *1st*, *day*, *night*, weekdays, and *may*, which occurs in 14 % of the tweets (mostly the modal verb, as in the frequent bigram *may not*), far more than in ordinary English. The tweets were gathered with keyword queries around named events and dates (sports games, concerts, film releases, elections), and annotators judged the sentiment towards that topic. Differences still appear: *not*, *no* and *don't* are two to three times more frequent in negative tweets than in the others (e.g. *not*: 134 per 1 000 negative tweets vs 64 neutral and 43 positive), which justifies keeping negations out of the stop-word list; *EMOPOS* (positive emoticons), *good* and *can't wait* rank high in positive tweets. Among bigrams, negative tweets are dominated by news topics (*boko haram*, *charlie hebdo*, *saudi arabia*), positive ones by entertainment and leisure (*ant man*, *star wars*, *ice cream*, *friday night*).

## polar
The Delta TF-IDF ranking is much more informative than raw frequencies. Positive words are evaluative (*enjoying*, *excellent*, *amazing*, *gorgeous*, *lovely*, *congrats*, *exciting*), plus words linked to positive events (*annual*, *workshop*, *preview*, times such as *5pm*). Negative words combine evaluation (*horrible*, *worst*, *worse*, *racist*, *false*, *failed*) with violent or judicial news vocabulary (*manslaughter*, *hanged*, *terror*, *terrorist*, *prison*, *arrested*, *charges*, *syria*). Part of the polarity is therefore carried by the topic rather than by opinion words, which a classifier will exploit, and which may not transfer to a test set about other topics.

## tags
15-20 % of tweets contain a hashtag and about 30 % a mention, with little difference between classes. The most frequent tags are topical (#raw, #summerslam, #wwe for wrestling, #gopdebate, #tcot in politics) and handles of brands and media (@microsoft, @youtube, @foxnews). The hashtags are kept as words by the cleaner (the `#` is removed) and mentions are replaced by a single `USER` token, so these topics remain available to the models.

## markers
Exclamation marks are the clearest marker: present in 33 % of positive tweets versus 13 % of negative and neutral ones. Question marks are less frequent in positive tweets (9 % vs 14-15 %). URLs are more frequent in neutral tweets (33 %), consistent with news headlines and shared links. **After correction, ASCII emoticons are rare** (2-8 % of tweets instead of the 23-35 % reported before): they are most frequent in positive tweets (7.5 %), and their polarity (`:)` vs `:(`) is analysed in Task 3. The training file contains **no emoji at all** (they were apparently stripped when it was built), whereas 4-15 % of the test tweets contain one, another difference between the two files. All-caps words appear in about 20 % of tweets of every class (many are acronyms such as USA or NFL).

## summary
- The training set (50,306 tweets) is imbalanced: neutral 44.9 %, positive 39.6 %, negative 15.6 %. The test set is distributed differently (negative 32.3 %, positive 19.3 %) and comes from a later period (Nov.-Dec. 2016 vs 2011-2015), so macro-averaged metrics and class weights are needed in Task 2.
- The raw files contain encoding artefacts (CSV quotes, `’`-style escapes, HTML entities) in 37 % of the texts; they are now repaired by the loader. 87 texts carry contradictory labels and about 1.5 % of rows are repeated; the models use a deduplicated copy.
- Tweet length is almost identical across classes (ε² < 0.006).
- Frequent words mostly reflect the collection topics (dates, events). The improved Delta TF-IDF isolates opinion words (*excellent*, *amazing* vs *worst*, *horrible*) but also topic words (news of violence on the negative side), which the classifiers will use.
- Negations (*not*, *no*, *don't*) are two to three times more frequent in negative tweets: they must not be removed as stop words.
- Exclamation marks are strongly associated with positive tweets; emoticons are rare (the previous estimate was inflated by URLs) and emoji are absent from the training file but present in the test file.

These statistics describe associations in this dataset; they do not establish causes and do not automatically generalise beyond it.
